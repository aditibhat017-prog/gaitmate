RESEARCH & DEVELOPMENT

Understanding movement. Advancing rehabilitation.

Gait is more than the way we walk. It reflects the interaction of the nervous system, muscles, joints, balance, and overall physical function.

GaitMate's research explores how wearable sensing and digital analysis could help characterize changes in movement, support rehabilitation, and investigate opportunities for preventive care.

THE IMPORTANCE OF GAIT

Movement as a window into health.

Walking is a complex process requiring coordination between the brain, spinal cord, peripheral nerves, muscles, and musculoskeletal system.

Changes in walking speed, step length, symmetry, balance, and movement variability can provide information about physical function and, in some settings, neurological health.

Because many of these changes emerge gradually, repeated measurements may help researchers understand how movement evolves over time. Wearable technology offers a potential way to collect movement information outside traditional laboratory or clinical environments.

01

Walking Speed

A widely studied measure of mobility and functional performance that may change with disease, injury, and aging.

02

Gait Symmetry

Differences between left and right movement patterns may help characterize compensatory movement or impaired function.

03

Gait Variability

Changes in the consistency of steps and movement patterns can be relevant to balance, neurological function, and mobility research.

CLINICAL RESEARCH DIRECTIONS

Exploring gait across different conditions.

Although GaitMate began with ankle rehabilitation, our long-term research interests extend to conditions in which altered gait, mobility, or balance is an important clinical feature.

These are proposed research directions rather than validated applications of the current GaitMate system.

01 — NEURODEGENERATIVE DISORDERS

Parkinson's Disease

Parkinson's disease can affect walking speed, stride length, turning, and movement initiation. Some individuals experience freezing of gait, in which forward movement becomes temporarily difficult.

Future GaitMate research could investigate how wearable movement measurements characterize changes in gait over time.

02 — NEUROLOGICAL DISORDERS

Multiple Sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis can affect walking ability, balance, coordination, and endurance. Walking speed and other mobility measures are already used in research and clinical assessment of the condition.

GaitMate could explore complementary, longitudinal movement monitoring, building on existing approaches rather than replacing established assessments.

03 — ACQUIRED NEUROLOGICAL INJURY

Stroke Rehabilitation

Stroke can lead to weakness, asymmetric walking patterns, altered balance, and changes in weight distribution.

Future wearable systems could investigate gait symmetry, postural sway, and movement progression during rehabilitation.

04 — BRAIN & SPINAL INJURY

Traumatic Brain & Spinal Cord Injury

Traumatic brain injury can affect balance, coordination, and walking performance. Spinal cord injury may impair motor control, strength, sensation, and mobility.

Future research could investigate condition-appropriate wearable measurements to support movement assessment and rehabilitation tracking.

05 — NEUROMUSCULAR CONDITIONS

Cerebral Palsy

Cerebral palsy can affect muscle tone, posture, coordination, and gait. Walking patterns vary considerably between individuals.

Wearable monitoring could be investigated as a complementary method for studying functional movement outside structured assessments.

06 — NEUROMUSCULAR CONDITIONS

Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy

Duchenne muscular dystrophy causes progressive muscle weakness and may produce characteristic compensatory walking patterns, including a waddling gait.

Future research could explore how noninvasive movement measurements relate to changes in mobility and functional performance.

07 — HEALTHY AGING

Fall-Risk Assessment

Changes in walking speed, balance, step variability, and mobility may contribute to fall-risk assessment in older adults.

GaitMate's long-term vision is to investigate whether combinations of wearable movement features could help identify elevated fall risk before a fall occurs.

08 — MUSCULOSKELETAL REHABILITATION

Injury Recovery

Musculoskeletal injuries can produce pain-related compensation, altered loading, and asymmetrical movement.

GaitMate's initial ankle rehabilitation research provides a starting point for investigating future applications across other joints and injuries.

INITIAL RESEARCH

Where GaitMate began: lateral ankle sprains.

GaitMate originated as a wearable biosensing concept focused on lateral ankle sprain rehabilitation.

Lateral ankle sprains commonly involve the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL), with the calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) and posterior talofibular ligament (PTFL) also relevant to lateral ankle anatomy and more extensive injuries.

Our initial research investigated how localized wearable measurements might help characterize mechanical changes around these ligament regions during movement and rehabilitation.

The proposed nine-sensor architecture uses three sensing locations associated with each ligament region. This is a conceptual placement strategy, not yet a validated method for directly measuring ligament strain.

ATFL

Anterior Talofibular Ligament

Frequently involved in lateral ankle sprains and a major focus of the initial rehabilitation concept.

CFL

Calcaneofibular Ligament

Contributes to lateral ankle stability and is included in the proposed sensing-region design.

PTFL

Posterior Talofibular Ligament

Part of the lateral ligament complex and included in the initial multi-region sensing concept.

CURRENT DEVELOPMENT

Translating research into a working prototype.

GaitMate is currently progressing through early hardware and software development. Our work focuses on evaluating candidate sensors, refining wearable architecture, and developing tools to process and visualize movement-related data.

01

Sensor Exploration

Comparing stretch, strain, inertial, and muscle-activity sensing modalities for potential use in wearable movement monitoring.

02

Hardware Prototyping

Building early electronic prototypes and evaluating sensor response to movement and orientation.

03

Wearable Integration

Investigating sticker construction, adhesive materials, sensor placement, and integration of electronics into a compact wearable format.

04

Software Development

Developing the application interface, data visualization, movement-event detection, and rehabilitation feedback concepts.

05

Bench Testing

Planning controlled tests of sensor response, repeatability, noise, stability, and mechanical performance.

06

Future Validation

Designing future studies to assess wearable measurement accuracy, movement characterization, and condition-specific applications.

RESEARCH ROADMAP

Our next questions.

01

Can we reliably capture movement?

Characterize sensor response, repeatability, placement sensitivity, and signal quality during controlled activities.

02

Which measurements are meaningful?

Investigate which features, including asymmetry and movement variability, are informative for specific rehabilitation research questions.

03

Can we monitor changes over time?

Explore longitudinal measurements and how movement patterns vary throughout recovery.

04

Can we identify emerging mobility risks?

Investigate whether validated combinations of gait features could contribute to future predictive models for instability and fall risk.

RESEARCH STATUS

An evolving research platform.

GaitMate is an early-stage research and engineering project. The wearable system, software features, and expanded clinical applications are in different stages of conceptual design and prototype development.

The platform has not been clinically validated for diagnosis, treatment decisions, fall-risk prediction, or disease-specific monitoring. Future research will require technical characterization and appropriate clinical studies.

SCIENTIFIC FOUNDATION

Research & references.

GaitMate's research direction draws on literature in gait biomechanics, lateral ankle injury rehabilitation, wearable sensing, neurological movement disorders, and fall-risk assessment.

We are developing a curated bibliography to document the evidence informing our sensing architecture, analysis methods, and future research questions.
